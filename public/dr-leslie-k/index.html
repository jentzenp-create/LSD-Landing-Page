Turning Dr. Leslie's homepage visitors into booked consultations

Executive summary

The message is right. The mechanics are getting in the way.

This page does the hardest part well: it names a specific person with a specific problem — women navigating midlife — and speaks to them with real empathy. What's undermining it is mechanical. The main call to action isn't a button, most of the phone links are broken, and the testimonials are set in a typeface almost nobody can read.

A visitor lands, reads a genuinely good headline and subhead, and then reaches "Book Your Free Consultation" — which is styled as a heading with an underline, not a button. The only elements on the entire page rendered as actual buttons are the cookie-consent controls. Below that, the same "Book a Free Consultation / or call" block repeats seven times verbatim, and six of those seven phone links contain a typo that makes them fail to dial when tapped on a phone.

Meanwhile the assets that would earn trust in a health context — warm client testimonials and a "Certified Wellcoach" credential — are either illegible or buried in the footer. None of this needs a rebuild. The fixes below are ordered so the mechanical breakages, which are cheap to repair, ship first.

Zone 1 · Above the fold

01A strong message with nothing to click

The copy here is doing its job. The problem is that the action it asks for doesn't look like an action, and nothing on screen yet tells a health-conscious visitor why to trust this person.

1
The primary CTA is a text link, not a buttonHigh

"Book Your Free Consultation" is marked up as a heading wrapped around a link (<h3><a>) with no button styling. Visitors scan for buttons; an underlined heading reads as a section title, not something to press. Tellingly, the only true buttons on the page belong to the cookie-consent notice. FixRender every consultation CTA as a real, high-contrast button with padding, a solid fill, and a hover state — so the single most important action on the page finally looks like one.

2
"Book a Call" in the nav is the only clickable-looking CTAMedium

The header item reads as the actionable element, which quietly competes with the hero's own offer and splits attention between two paths to the same outcome. FixMake the nav item a visually distinct button and point it at the same scheduler, so the header and hero reinforce one path instead of implying two.

4
No credentials or proof above the foldHigh

For a health service, credibility is the conversion. Nothing above the fold says what kind of doctor "Dr. Leslie" is, what she's certified in, or that other women have had results. The "Certified Wellcoach" badge exists — it's just at the very bottom of the page (see finding 8). FixAdd a compact trust strip directly under the hero CTA: the certification badge, a one-line credential, and a short pull-quote from a client. It answers "can I trust her?" at the moment the question forms.

Zone 2 · The repeated call to action

02The same ask, seven times — and a broken phone number

Repeating the CTA down a long page is good practice. Repeating it word for word, with a phone link that doesn't work, is not.

3
Six of seven phone links fail to dialHigh

The hero link is correct (tel:+18055382218), but the six repeated "or call" links contain an extra digit — tel:+180553822108. On a phone, tapping those does not dial the practice. Every visitor who prefers calling over booking online, on the majority of the page's CTAs, hits a dead end. FixCorrect all six links to tel:+18055382218, then tap-test each one on an actual phone. This is the cheapest high-value fix on the page.

5
The identical CTA block repeats seven timesMedium

The same heading and phone line appear verbatim after nearly every section. Repetition without variation stops registering — and worse, it spends prime page real estate without adding a single new reason to book. FixKeep the CTA frequency but vary the framing to match the section above it — "Talk through your symptoms," "Ask about your specific condition," "See if we're a fit." Each becomes a fresh, contextual invitation rather than wallpaper.

Zone 3 · Social proof

03Excellent testimonials, rendered unreadable

The written content here is the most persuasive material on the entire site — a client describing a 65-pound loss, another describing renewed mobility. Almost no one will read it.

6
Testimonials are set in an illegible all-caps scriptHigh

The quotes are rendered in a small, light, all-capitals decorative face. All-caps removes the word shapes readers rely on, and the low contrast compounds it — a particular problem for an audience of women in midlife, where reading comfort matters more, not less. The most compelling proof on the site is functionally invisible. FixSet testimonials in the body typeface at normal sentence case, ~16–18px, at full contrast. Add each client's name and photo where permitted. This one change makes existing content persuasive again at zero content cost.

7
The strongest proof sits ~3,600px down the pageMedium

"Why Trust Dr. Leslie" is the section most likely to convert a hesitant visitor, and it arrives only after a long scroll — well past the point where most visitors decide. FixPromote one short, high-impact quote into the trust strip beneath the hero, and keep the full set here as supporting depth.

Zone 4 · Footer

04A credential doing no work

The one formal credibility marker on the page is placed where the fewest people will see it.

8
"Certified Wellcoach" badge is buried in the footerMedium

Third-party certification is exactly the reassurance a prospective health client looks for, and it sits below the disclaimer in the darkest part of the page, where the fewest visitors ever reach. FixMove the badge into the hero trust strip. Keep a copy in the footer if you like, but its job is at the top.

Beyond the zones

05Site-wide findings

Structural and trust issues that affect the whole page rather than one section.

A
The page has no H1 heading at allMedium

The homepage uses only H2, H3, and H4 tags — there is no top-level H1. Search engines use it as the page's primary topic signal, and screen-reader users rely on it to orient. Both are losing information the page already contains. FixPromote "Drug-Free Health Coaching for Women Navigating Midlife in California" to an H1. The copy is already right; only the tag needs to change.

B
Competing color blocks with no consistent action colorMedium

Sections alternate between magenta, olive, warm grey, and lavender. Because no single color is reserved for actions, nothing reads as "this is the thing to click," and the palette shifts attention rather than directing it. FixPick one accent reserved exclusively for CTAs and let the section colors act as quieter backgrounds behind it. Consistency is what makes a button feel like a button.

C
Booking opens an external scheduler in a new tabLow

CTAs jump to an external scheduling subdomain in a new tab. It works, but the visual and domain shift is a small confidence dip at the most fragile moment. FixBrand the scheduler page to match the site where the tool allows, so the handoff feels continuous.

D
Page title reads awkwardly in search resultsLow

The title tag is "A Natural Approach -to Healthy Living and Lifestyle Home" — a stray hyphen, odd spacing, and a trailing "Home." It's the first thing a searcher reads. FixRewrite to lead with the service and audience, e.g. "Drug-Free Health Coaching for Women in Midlife | Dr. Leslie K."

E
Logo is small and hard to readLow

The wordmark in the top-left renders as a faint script that's difficult to make out, weakening brand recall on arrival. FixIncrease its size and contrast, or pair it with the practice name set in text.

Recommended sequence

06A three-phase rollout

Ordered so the mechanical breakages — fast and cheap to repair — are fixed before any design work begins.

Phase 1 · Repairs — days, not weeks

Fix what's actually broken

  • Correct the six malformed phone links and tap-test on a real device (Finding 3)
  • Turn every consultation CTA into a real button (Findings 1, 2)
  • Reset testimonials to a legible face at sentence case and full contrast (Finding 6)
  • Promote the hero headline to an H1 (Finding A)
Phase 2 · Trust & messaging — 2–3 weeks

Earn the click sooner

  • Build a trust strip under the hero: certification badge, credential line, client quote (Findings 4, 7, 8)
  • Vary the repeated CTA copy so each instance adds a reason (Finding 5)
  • Commit to one reserved action color across the page (Finding B)
  • Rewrite the page title; brand the scheduler handoff (Findings C, D)
Phase 3 · Measure & iterate — ongoing

Track what works, then refine

  • Confirm analytics captures CTA clicks and completed bookings, so consultation volume is attributable
  • Watch which CTA position and wording earns the most bookings, and iterate
  • Test the hero headline against one alternative that leads with the outcome rather than the method

The site's platform supports all of the above, including proper A/B testing and behavioral tools should you want them later.
